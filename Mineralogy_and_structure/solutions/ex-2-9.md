## Statement

> How much does the specific surface of a cubic particle of side \(L\) changes when its side is reduced by half?

Self-contained. The governing definition is printed on the same slide, in the `defBox` immediately above the statement: $SSA = \dfrac{A}{V\rho}$.

## Given / Find

- A cube of side $L$, made of a solid of particle density $\rho$; the definition $SSA = \dfrac{A}{V\rho}$
- The particle is then replaced by one of side $L_f = L/2$
- **Find:** the ratio $SSA_f / SSA_o$ — i.e. by what factor the specific surface changes

## Assumptions

**The particle stays a cube — only its size changes.** "Reduced by half" is read as the *side* being halved, which the statement says explicitly, so the shape factor is held constant and the whole result is a size effect. Worth recording because the same words could be read as halving the *volume* or the *mass*, which would give $SSA_f/SSA_o = 2^{1/3} = 1.26$ instead of $2$. The reading matters, so it becomes a red note on the slide.

**$\rho$ is the density of the solid mineral, not a bulk density, and it does not change when the particle is broken.** Breaking a grain makes more grains, not a different mineral. This is what lets $\rho$ cancel, and it is the reason the answer is a pure number rather than something that depends on which soil you are holding.

**$SSA$ is defined per unit *mass*** — that is what the $\rho$ in the denominator is doing. Some texts define specific surface per unit *volume* ($A/V$, units $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$). Both scale as $1/L$, so the factor of $2$ is the same either way, but the units differ and students who half-remember one definition write the other. Recorded so the slide can print the units explicitly.

## Solution

Specific surface is a ratio of an area to a mass, so it carries one net length in the denominator; halving a length therefore has to double it, and the algebra below is really just a check on that. The route is to build $SSA$ from its definition twice — once at side $L$, once at side $L/2$ — and divide.

1. Surface area of the original cube — six faces, $A_o = 6L^2$
2. Volume and mass of the original cube — $V_o = L^3$, so its mass is $m_o = V_o\rho = L^3\rho$
3. Original specific surface — $SSA_o = \dfrac{A_o}{V_o\rho} = \dfrac{6L^2}{L^3\rho} = \dfrac{6}{L\rho}$
4. The halved particle — $L_f = \dfrac{L}{2}$, so $A_f = 6\left(\dfrac{L}{2}\right)^2 = \dfrac{3L^2}{2}$ and $V_f = \left(\dfrac{L}{2}\right)^3 = \dfrac{L^3}{8}$
5. New specific surface — $SSA_f = \dfrac{A_f}{V_f\rho} = \dfrac{3L^2/2}{(L^3/8)\rho} = \dfrac{12}{L\rho}$
6. Ratio — $\dfrac{SSA_f}{SSA_o} = \dfrac{12/(L\rho)}{6/(L\rho)} = \mathbf{2}$, so the specific surface **doubles** — a $100\ \%$ increase — and neither $L$ nor $\rho$ survives into the answer

The general statement behind it, which the slide should say once: for any shape held geometrically similar, $A \propto L^2$ and $V \propto L^3$, so $SSA \propto 1/L$. Halving any linear dimension doubles the specific surface, and the cube is only the easiest case to write down.

## Verification

```python
from fractions import Fraction as F

SSA = lambda L, rho: 6*L**2 / (L**3 * rho)          # cube: A/(V.rho), coded from the definition only

# 1. the algebra, exactly, over a sweep of L and rho -- the ratio must be 2 for every pair
print("SSA_f / SSA_0 for a cube whose side is halved, exact rational arithmetic")
print("      L        rho        SSA_0          SSA_f       ratio    6/(L.rho)?  12/(L.rho)?")
ok = True
for L in [F(1), F(1, 2), F(3), F(7, 10), F(100)]:
    for rho in [F(1), F(2650), F(27, 10)]:
        s0, sf = SSA(L, rho), SSA(L/2, rho)
        r = sf/s0
        ok &= (r == 2) and (s0 == 6/(L*rho)) and (sf == 12/(L*rho))
        if rho == F(2650):
            print("  %7s %8s %14s %14s %8s %10s %10s"
                  % (L, rho, s0, sf, r, s0 == 6/(L*rho), sf == 12/(L*rho)))
print("  ratio == 2 and SSA_0 == 6/(L.rho) and SSA_f == 12/(L.rho) for all 15 (L,rho) pairs:", ok)
print()

# 2. general scaling SSA(kL)/SSA(L) = 1/k  -> SSA is inversely proportional to the side
print("general side kL:   SSA(kL)/SSA(L) should equal 1/k")
for k in [F(1, 4), F(1, 2), F(1), F(2), F(10)]:
    print("   k = %6s   ratio = %8s   1/k = %8s   agree = %s"
          % (k, SSA(k*F(5), F(2650))/SSA(F(5), F(2650)), 1/k,
             SSA(k*F(5), F(2650))/SSA(F(5), F(2650)) == 1/k))
print()

# 3. mass-conserving route: one cube of side L splits into n cubes of side L/2
L = F(1)
n = L**3 / (L/2)**3
A_tot = n * 6 * (L/2)**2
print("mass-conserving cross-check (same solid, cut into 8):")
print("   n = V_0/V_f =", n, " cubes;  total volume =", n*(L/2)**3, "= V_0 =", L**3, "(mass unchanged)")
print("   total area  =", A_tot, " vs A_0 =", 6*L**2, " -> ratio =", A_tot/(6*L**2))
print("   same factor 2, reached without ever writing rho -> the answer is geometry, not material")
print()

# 4. the error modes the scaffolding is built against
print("error modes:")
print("   A_f/A_0 =", (6*(L/2)**2)/(6*L**2), "  <- 'area drops to a quarter, so SSA drops'  (WRONG)")
print("   V_f/V_0 =", ((L/2)**3)/(L**3),     "  <- volume drops to an eighth")
print("   (A_f/A_0)/(V_f/V_0) =", ((6*(L/2)**2)/(6*L**2))/(((L/2)**3)/(L**3)),
      " <- volume falls faster than area; that ratio IS the answer")
print()

# 5. units and magnitude sanity, SI
print("units:  [A]/([V].[rho]) = m^2 / (m^3 . kg/m^3) = m^2/kg  -> area per unit MASS")
rho_q = 2650.0                                            # kg/m3, quartz
for name, side in [("1 mm sand grain", 1e-3), ("0.5 mm  (halved)", 0.5e-3),
                   ("0.002 mm clay size", 2e-6), ("0.001 mm (halved)", 1e-6)]:
    s = 6/(side*rho_q)
    print("   %-19s L = %8.1e m   SSA = %10.2f m2/kg = %8.4f m2/g" % (name, side, s, s/1000))
print("   measured SSA (Mitchell & Soga 2005): kaolinite 10-20, illite 80-100, montmorillonite ~800 m2/g")
print("   -> the cube model gives only ~1.1 m2/g at 2 um, so PLATE shape, not size alone, makes clays")
print("      active. The 1/L scaling this example proves is still exact for any self-similar shape.")
```

Every expression was rebuilt from the definition $SSA = A/(V\rho)$ in exact rational arithmetic rather than transcribed, and the factor of $2$ was produced three independent ways. First, directly, over fifteen $(L,\rho)$ pairs spanning three orders of magnitude in size and including a unit density — the ratio is exactly $2$ in all fifteen, which is the machine statement of "$L$ and $\rho$ cancel". Second, through the general scaling $SSA(kL)/SSA(L) = 1/k$, checked at five values of $k$ including $k>1$, so the result is not an artefact of the particular factor one half. Third, by the mass-conserving route: one cube of side $L$ cut into the $8$ cubes of side $L/2$ that fill the same volume has total area $12L^2$ against the original $6L^2$, again a factor of $2$, and that route never writes $\rho$ at all.

Units check dimensionally at every step, not only at the end: $A$ is $\mathrm{m^2}$, $V\rho$ is $\mathrm{m^3 \cdot kg/m^3} = \mathrm{kg}$, so $SSA$ is $\mathrm{m^2/kg}$ throughout, and the final answer is a ratio of two quantities in $\mathrm{m^2/kg}$ and therefore dimensionless — which is the check that catches a student who reports "$2\ \mathrm{m^2/kg}$".

Magnitude sanity is the one place this example can mislead, so it was checked deliberately. A $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ quartz cube gives $SSA = 0.0023\ \mathrm{m^2/g}$ and a $2\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ cube gives $1.13\ \mathrm{m^2/g}$, against measured values of $10\text{–}20\ \mathrm{m^2/g}$ for kaolinite and roughly $800\ \mathrm{m^2/g}$ for montmorillonite. The cube model is therefore two to three orders of magnitude low for real clay: size alone does not explain clay behaviour, plate *shape* does. The $1/L$ scaling this example establishes is still exact — it holds for any shape held similar — but the numbers must not be presented as predictions of real clay surface areas. That is a note, not a correction.

## Scaffolding decisions

**Six steps, one physical quantity each, all on one part.** The deck gives this example a single `ProblemBox` slide, and six steps fit it comfortably; no `data-part` is needed.

**The two "before" steps (1 and 2) are almost entirely black.** $A_o = 6L^2$ and $V_o = L^3$ are recall, not decisions, and pre-printing them puts the student straight at the substitution. Only the numbers $6$ and the exponents sit in blue, so that a student who writes $A = L^2$ for a cube is caught on line one rather than three steps later.

**Step 3 blanks the cancellation, not the setup.** $\dfrac{6L^2}{L^3\rho}$ is pre-printed and $\dfrac{6}{L\rho}$ is the blue fill. Cancelling $L^2$ against $L^3$ to leave one $L$ downstairs *is* the physics of this example — it is the reason specific surface goes as $1/L$ — so it is exactly the operation worth making them perform.

**Step 4 blanks both $A_f$ and $V_f$, and deliberately keeps them on one line.** The whole example turns on $\left(\tfrac12\right)^2$ against $\left(\tfrac12\right)^3$, and seeing $\tfrac{3L^2}{2}$ and $\tfrac{L^3}{8}$ written side by side is what makes the next step obvious. Splitting them into two steps would hide the comparison.

**Step 6 is the result line and carries the only `.ans`: the factor $2$.** It is short, memorable and typeable, which is what a blank should be. The verbal form — "the specific surface doubles" — is pre-printed black beside it, so the blank is the number rather than the sentence.

**No blank is longer than a few characters anywhere in the example.** Everything blanked is either a monomial or an integer, which is what the ebook's plain-text blanks handle well.

Red notes, at most one per step. (1) a cube has six faces, not four — the commonest slip, and it silently scales the whole answer. (3) $\rho$ is the density of the *solid*, and breaking a grain does not change it, which is why it cancels. (4) $\left(\tfrac12\right)^2 = \tfrac14$ but $\left(\tfrac12\right)^3 = \tfrac18$; both fall, and the answer is which falls faster. (5) $SSA$ is area per unit **mass**, so its units are $\mathrm{m^2/kg}$ — a definition using $A/V$ instead gives $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and the same factor of $2$. (6) "reduced by half" means the *side*; halving the volume instead would give $2^{1/3} = 1.26$, not $2$.

**One note needs a `data-slide` short form.** The step 6 note is the assumption note and the full text is worth keeping for the book; the slide gets "&ldquo;Halved&rdquo; means the **side**. Halving the *volume* would give $2^{1/3}\approx1.26$ instead."

**No answer is restated in a note.** The step 4 note names the two fractions $\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac18$ but not their quotient, which is the answer; the step 6 note gives the competing value $1.26$, not $2$.

**The `findingBox` on the existing slide stays.** "The smaller the particle size, the higher the specific surface" is the takeaway, sits outside the `ProblemBox`, and reads correctly as a conclusion drawn after the solution rather than as part of it.
