Alluvial deposits

These are soils transported and deposited in a . Alluvial deposits can be complex due to changes in flow velocity during flood and droughts cycles.
  • Particles are usually rounded.
  • Usually uniform gradation (soil particles have similar size).
  • They will often consist of coarse-sized material.

Estuarine deposits

Soils transported and deposited in estuaries. Estuarine deposits are highly complex due to interactions of river flow, tides, and waves.
  • Layering or mixtures of sand, silts, clay, and organic matter.
  • Can form deltas.
  • Very soft fine grained deposits.

Lacustrine/marine soils

Two distinct types of deposits with a similar origin: both settle in near-zero-velocity flows. Soils deposited offshore are marine soils; those deposited in lakes are lacustrine soils.
  • Usually fine soils and/or organic matter.
  • Salt water influences the clay chemistry of marine sediments.

Glacial deposits

Soils transported by glaciers and deposited during thaw processes; they are very heterogeneous and complex.
  • Mixed particle sizes, from clay to boulders.
  • Made of angular clasts.
  • Old glacial deposits are known as till.

Eolian deposits

Soils transported by wind. Examples: coastal dunes with very rounded particles, and loess, which has a weak structure prone to wetting collapse.
  • Common in arid regions and some coastal areas.
  • Sand dunes migrate quickly.
  • Loess is made of wind-blown silt.
