## Statement

> For the data attached, calculate \(N\) and \(N_{60}\). The automatic hammer was used. Assume \(C_B=1.0\), \(C_S=1.0\), and \(C_R=1.0\)

Figure-dependent. "The data attached" is a boring log drawn in `Plots.svg`, and the row to use is identified only by a red ellipse and a hand-drawn arrow labelled "These data is used". Both the depth scale and the circled row were decoded from the rendered figure rather than eyeballed — see Verification.

The two definitions the example runs on are printed earlier in the deck and should be treated as available: $N = N_f + N_s$ from the SPT `defBox` (deck line 525), and $N_{60} = N \times C_E \times C_B \times C_S \times C_R$ from the correction slide (deck line 622), with the hammer tables on deck lines 594–611 and 635–653.

## Given / Find

- Boring log `Plots.svg`, circled row: three $15\ \mathrm{cm}$ increments reading $5$, $7$, $11$ blows, at a depth of roughly $2.7\ \mathrm{m}$
- Automatic hammer, so $E_E = 0.78$ and $C_E = 1.3$ from the deck's two tables
- $C_B = 1.0$, $C_S = 1.0$, $C_R = 1.0$, all fixed by the statement
- Standard hammer: $W = 140\ \mathrm{lb}$ falling $h = 30\ \mathrm{in}$ ($63.5\ \mathrm{kg}$ through $760\ \mathrm{mm}$), so $E_{max} = 4200\ \mathrm{lb\text{-}in}$
- **Find:** $N$ and $N_{60}$

## Assumptions

**The row to use is the circled one, $5\ |\ 7\ |\ 11$.** The figure marks it with a red ellipse and the annotation "These data is used", so this is stated rather than chosen — but it has to be recorded, because the log carries eleven other SPT rows and the example is meaningless without knowing which. Conveniently the row immediately below is also $5\ |\ 7\ |\ 11$, so a student who misreads the arrow by one row still gets the right answer; that is luck, not design.

**The three columns are the three penetration increments in driving order, so the seating drive is $5$ and $N_f = 7$, $N_s = 11$.** The log's header reads "SPT Counts / 15 cm | 15 cm | 15 cm" left to right, and the deck's own definition drives the sampler in three $150\ \mathrm{mm}$ increments. This is the single place the example can go wrong silently: summing all three gives $23$, which is a plausible-looking blow count and — by coincidence in this problem — *also* the final answer for $N_{60}$. A student who adds all three and stops will appear to have the right answer for the wrong reason.

**$C_E = 1.3$ is read from the deck's correction-factor table for the automatic hammer, and it is not independent of the efficiency table.** $C_E = E_E / 0.60 = 0.78/0.60 = 1.30$ exactly, and the same holds for the other two hammers ($0.45/0.60 = 0.75$, $0.60/0.60 = 1.00$). The deck prints two tables that are one table. Recorded because it turns $C_E$ from something to memorise into something to derive, and it becomes a red note.

**$C_R = 1.0$ is used because the statement says so, not because it is what a rod-length table would give here.** The circled test is at about $2.7\ \mathrm{m}$ depth, so with $1$–$1.5\ \mathrm{m}$ of rod above the collar the driven length is roughly $3$–$4\ \mathrm{m}$, where Youd et al. (2001) give $C_R = 0.75$–$0.85$. That would put $N_{60}$ at $18$–$20$ rather than $23$. The statement's instruction governs and the answer stands at $23$, but this is a real simplification with a real cost, and it must appear as a red note rather than pass unmentioned.

**$N$ and $N_{60}$ are reported as whole blows.** $N_{60} = 23.4$ is rounded to $23$, matching the deck. Blow counts are integers by measurement, and a tenth of a blow does not exist; carrying the decimal would suggest a precision the test does not have.

**"blows/ft" and "blows per $300\ \mathrm{mm}$" are the same number here.** The log's increments are metric $150\ \mathrm{mm}$, so the last two span $300\ \mathrm{mm}$, which the deck's definition slide equates to $12\ \mathrm{in}$. No conversion is applied anywhere; the unit label is a naming choice, not an arithmetic step.

## Solution

The whole example is one idea: the raw blow count is a property of *the rig as much as the soil*, so it is not comparable between crews until it is put on a common energy footing. Steps 1 and 2 get the number the field crew wrote down; steps 3 to 5 convert it into the number an engineer can correlate against.

1. Which increments count — the circled row gives seating $= 5$, $N_f = 7$, $N_s = 11$ blows per $15\ \mathrm{cm}$; the seating drive is **discarded**, because that soil was disturbed by drilling and has lost its confinement
2. Field blow count — $N = N_f + N_s = 7 + 11 = \mathbf{18}$ blows/ft, the blows to drive the last $300\ \mathrm{mm}$
3. Hammer energy — the standard hammer delivers, in theory, $E_{max} = Wh = 140 \times 30 = 4200\ \mathrm{lb\text{-}in}$; an automatic hammer is $E_E = 0.78$ efficient, so it actually delivers $E_h = E_E E_{max} = 0.78 \times 4200 = 3276\ \mathrm{lb\text{-}in}$
4. Energy correction factor — the reference is $60\ \%$ efficiency, so $C_E = \dfrac{E_E}{0.60} = \dfrac{0.78}{0.60} = 1.3$; this hammer drives harder than the reference, so the correction is greater than one
5. Corrected blow count — $N_{60} = N \times C_E \times C_B \times C_S \times C_R = 18 \times 1.3 \times 1.0 \times 1.0 \times 1.0 = 23.4 \approx \mathbf{23}$ blows/ft

Step 4 is the step worth dwelling on out loud. $C_E > 1$ looks backwards to students the first time: a *more* efficient hammer produces *fewer* field blows, so its blow count has to be scaled *up* to represent what the $60\ \%$ reference hammer would have recorded. The correction restores the energy, it does not reward the equipment.

```python
from PIL import Image
import numpy as np

# ---- 1. locate the circled row on the boring log, from the figure itself ----
# Plots.svg rendered to 900x1440 px. The depth column's horizontal rules give the
# scale; the red ellipse the author drew ("These data is used") gives the row.
im = np.array(Image.open('Plots.png').convert('L'))
rgb = np.array(Image.open('Plots.png').convert('RGB')).astype(int)


def rules(x0, x1, thr):
    prof = (im < 140)[:, x0:x1].sum(1)
    out, run = [], []
    for r, v in enumerate(prof):
        if v >= thr:
            run.append(r)
        elif run:
            out.append(sum(run) / len(run)); run = []
    return out


rr = [r for r in rules(5, 85, 60) if 200 < r < 1440]
step = (rr[-1] - rr[0]) / round((rr[-1] - rr[0]) / 150.6)
depth = lambda y: 1.00 + (y - rr[0]) / step
print("depth scale from the log's own rules: %.2f px per metre" % step)
print("   rules at y =", [round(r) for r in rr], "-> depths",
      ["%.2f" % depth(r) for r in rr])

red = (rgb[:, :, 0] > 170) & (rgb[:, :, 1] < 90) & (rgb[:, :, 2] < 90)
ys, xs = np.nonzero(red)
print("   red ellipse spans y %d..%d -> depth %.2f..%.2f m, centre %.2f m"
      % (ys.min(), ys.max(), depth(ys.min()), depth(ys.max()),
         depth((ys.min() + ys.max()) / 2)))
print()

# ---- 2. the three increments, read off the circled row ----
seating, Nf, Ns = 5, 7, 11
N = Nf + Ns
print("SPT increments (15 cm each): seating=%d  N_f=%d  N_s=%d" % (seating, Nf, Ns))
print("   N = N_f + N_s = %d + %d = %d blows/ft   (last two increments = 30 cm = 12 in = 1 ft)"
      % (Nf, Ns, N))
print("   seating blows are DISCARDED, not added: N would be %d if they were" % (seating + Nf + Ns))
print("   refusal check: no increment >= 50, so this is a valid N, not 'N = 50+':",
      max(seating, Nf, Ns) < 50)
print()

# ---- 3. hammer energy ----
W, drop = 140.0, 30.0                      # lb, in  (deck: 63.5 kg falling 760 mm)
Emax = W * drop
EE = 0.78                                  # automatic hammer, deck's efficiency table
Eh = EE * Emax
print("theoretical energy  E_max = W x h = %.0f lb x %.0f in = %.0f lb-in   (deck prints 4200 lb-in: %s)"
      % (W, drop, Emax, Emax == 4200))
print("   SI cross-check    63.5 kg x 9.81 m/s2 x 0.76 m = %.1f J ;  %.0f lb-in = %.1f J   agree to %.1f %%"
      % (63.5 * 9.81 * 0.76, Emax, Emax * 0.1129848,
         abs(63.5 * 9.81 * 0.76 - Emax * 0.1129848) / (63.5 * 9.81 * 0.76) * 100))
print("   delivered energy  E_h = E_E x E_max = %.2f x %.0f = %.0f lb-in = %.0f J"
      % (EE, Emax, Eh, Eh * 0.1129848))
print()

# ---- 4. energy correction factor ----
CE_derived = EE / 0.60
print("energy correction  C_E = E_E / 0.60 = %.2f / 0.60 = %.4f -> %.1f" % (EE, CE_derived, CE_derived))
print("   the deck's TWO tables are the same table -- C_E is just E_E normalised to 60 %:")
for name, e, c in [("Donut", 0.45, 0.75), ("Safety", 0.60, 1.0), ("Automatic", 0.78, 1.3)]:
    print("      %-10s E_E=%.2f  E_E/0.60=%.4f  deck's C_E=%.2f   agree=%s"
          % (name, e, e / 0.60, c, abs(e / 0.60 - c) < 0.005))
print()

# ---- 5. N60 ----
CE, CB, CS, CR = 1.3, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0        # C_B, C_S, C_R fixed by the statement
N60 = N * CE * CB * CS * CR
print("N_60 = N x C_E x C_B x C_S x C_R = %d x %.1f x %.1f x %.1f x %.1f = %.1f -> %d blows/ft"
      % (N, CE, CB, CS, CR, N60, round(N60)))
print()

# ---- 6. what the statement's C_R = 1.0 costs -----------------------------
# Youd et al. (2001) / Skempton (1986) rod-length correction
CR_table = [(3, 0.75), (4, 0.80), (6, 0.85), (10, 0.95), (30, 1.00)]
z = depth((ys.min() + ys.max()) / 2)
print("rod-length correction, if it were NOT assumed 1.0 (test at ~%.1f m depth):" % z)
for rod, cr in CR_table:
    v = N * CE * cr
    print("   rod <= %4.0f m -> C_R = %.2f -> N_60 = %.1f -> %2d blows/ft" % (rod, cr, v, round(v)))
print("   plausible rod length here is depth + ~1-1.5 m stickup ~= %.1f-%.1f m -> C_R ~ 0.75-0.85"
      % (z, z + 1.5))
print("   so the statement's C_R = 1.0 raises the reported N_60 from ~18-20 to %d" % round(N60))
print()

# ---- 7. magnitude / consistency sanity -----------------------------------
print("sanity checks")
print("   deck's clay table: 8-15 stiff, 15-30 very stiff.  N = %d -> 'very stiff'" % N)
print("   the log calls this layer 'Firm to Stiff ... lean CLAY with coarse sand and some gravel'")
print("   -> the blow count reads one class HIGH for the described consistency; gravel in the")
print("      shoe is the usual cause, and it is exactly the deck's 'crude number / high variability'")
print("      limitation. Worth a note, not a correction.")
print("   units: N and N_60 are blows per foot (per 300 mm); C_E, C_B, C_S, C_R are dimensionless,")
print("      so N_60 carries the same unit as N. Energies in lb-in throughout, cross-checked in J.")
print("   the row below the circled one is also 5 | 7 | 11, so the reading is robust either way.")
```

The two answers, $N = 18$ and $N_{60} = 23$, were produced from the definitions rather than transcribed from the deck, and they then agreed with the deck exactly, which is the cross-check.

Nothing was read off the picture by eye. `Plots.svg` was rendered to a $900\times1440$ bitmap and both the depth scale and the marked row were recovered from it numerically: the log's own horizontal rules in the depth column come out at a uniform $150.62$ px per metre and land on exactly $1.00, 4.00, 5.00, \ldots, 9.00\ \mathrm{m}$, which is what validates the scale before it is used for anything. The red ellipse the author drew then spans $2.32$–$3.00\ \mathrm{m}$ with its centre at $2.66\ \mathrm{m}$ — that is what fixes the test depth used in the rod-length discussion below, and it is a measurement rather than an impression.

The hammer energy was checked in two unit systems, because this is the one place in the example where a conversion could hide. $E_{max} = 140\ \mathrm{lb} \times 30\ \mathrm{in} = 4200\ \mathrm{lb\text{-}in}$ reproduces the value the deck prints on its efficiency slide, and the deck's SI statement of the same hammer, $63.5\ \mathrm{kg}$ falling $760\ \mathrm{mm}$, gives $473.4\ \mathrm{J}$ against $474.5\ \mathrm{J}$ for $4200\ \mathrm{lb\text{-}in}$ — agreement to $0.2\ \%$, the difference being the rounding in $63.5\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $760\ \mathrm{mm}$. The two ways of stating the standard hammer are the same hammer.

The relationship $C_E = E_E/0.60$ was tested against all three of the deck's hammers, not just the one this problem uses, and it reproduces the printed correction factors exactly in every case. So the second table is derivable from the first, and step 4 can be taught rather than looked up.

Units are consistent at every step, not only at the end: $C_E$, $C_B$, $C_S$ and $C_R$ are all dimensionless ratios, so $N_{60}$ carries the same unit as $N$ — blows per foot, equivalently blows per $300\ \mathrm{mm}$. Energies stay in $\mathrm{lb\text{-}in}$ throughout, converted to joules only for the cross-check. The refusal condition was also tested: no increment reaches $50$ blows, so this is a valid $N$ and not a "$N = 50+$" record, which the deck's field-notes slide requires be checked before the counts are summed.

One magnitude check does not sit comfortably, and it is worth knowing about rather than hiding. $N = 18$ falls in the deck's own "very stiff" band for clays ($15$–$30$), while the log describes this layer as "Firm to Stiff... lean CLAY with coarse sand and some gravel". The blow count reads one consistency class high for the material described. Gravel jamming the sampler shoe is the usual cause and is exactly the "crude number / high variability" limitation the deck lists two slides later, so this is a teaching point rather than an error in the data — but a student who correlates $N_{60} = 23$ straight to an undrained strength will over-predict, and the solution should say so once.

## Scaffolding decisions

**Five steps, one part, one slide.** The deck gives this example a single slide whose `ProblemBox` is pinned at `height: 480px`, so the solution has a hard vertical budget and five steps is what fits. It is below the usual six to eight, but the example genuinely obtains only five quantities and padding it would show.

**Step 1 obtains no number and is the most important step in the example.** It is where the seating drive is thrown away. Pre-printing the three increments in black and blanking only which two are summed forces the decision to be made explicitly, instead of a student adding whatever is in the row. The trap is unusually cruel here — summing all three gives $23$, which is the *final answer* to the second part of the question, so a wrong method lands on a right-looking number twice over. That coincidence is the single strongest argument for making this a numbered step rather than a note.

**Step 2 blanks the substitution and the result, both short.** $7+11$ and $18$ are two characters and two characters; both are worth writing and neither is tedious to type into an ebook blank.

**Step 3 pre-prints $E_{max} = Wh$ symbolically and blanks the two substitutions.** $140\times30$ and $0.78\times4200$ are the only arithmetic in the example that is not a one-digit sum, and they are what make the efficiency concrete. The delivered energy $E_h$ is not asked for by the statement; it earns its step by being the physical quantity that $C_E$ is built from, and without it step 4 is a table lookup with no meaning attached.

**Step 4 blanks $E_E/0.60$ rather than just the value $1.3$.** Writing the ratio is what shows where the subscript in $N_{60}$ comes from, and it is the difference between a student who can correct for a hammer the deck's table does not list and one who cannot. The result $1.3$ is a second short blue fill.

**Step 5 is the result line and carries the only green `.ans`: $N_{60} = 23$.** The full five-factor product is pre-printed in black with all four corrections substituted, because three of them are $1.0$ by instruction and blanking a $1.0$ teaches nothing; the blue fill is $18\times1.3$ and the green is the rounded answer. $N = 18$ from step 2 is *also* an answer the statement asks for, so step 2 should carry an `.ans` as well rather than a `.fill` — this example has two green values, which is unusual but correct, since the question says "calculate $N$ and $N_{60}$".

Red notes, at most one per step. (1) the seating blows are discarded because that soil was disturbed by drilling and has lost confinement — and adding all three here gives $23$, which *looks* like an answer. (2) $N$ is blows per foot, i.e. per the last $300\ \mathrm{mm}$, not per the full $450\ \mathrm{mm}$ driven. (3) $E_{max}$ is the *theoretical* free-fall energy $Wh$; no real hammer delivers it, which is the entire reason corrections exist. (4) $C_E > 1$ is not a bonus for good equipment — an efficient hammer needs fewer blows, so its count is scaled **up** to match what the $60\ \%$ reference would have recorded. (5) $C_R = 1.0$ is given, but at this depth a published table gives $0.75$–$0.85$.

**Two notes need `data-slide` short forms.** Step 1: "Seating blows are **discarded** — that soil was disturbed by drilling. Adding all three gives 23, which is *not* $N$." Step 5: "Assumed by the statement: $C_R=1.0$. At $\approx3$ m rod length a table gives $0.75$–$0.85$, i.e. $N_{60}\approx18$–$20$."

**The gravel caveat goes in a `findingBox` beside the box, not in a step or a note.** It is a conclusion about the result rather than part of obtaining it, the note budget for step 5 is already spent on $C_R$, and it is the natural hand-off to the "Strengths and Limitations" slide two slides later.

**No answer is restated in a note.** Step 1's note names $23$ as the *wrong* sum — which is the same digits as the right final answer — so this one needs checking when the build's leak detector runs in Phase 2. It is pedagogically the most valuable sentence in the example and I would keep it even if the checker flags it, but you should decide that rather than have me decide it silently.
