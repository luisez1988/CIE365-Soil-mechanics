## Assumptions

## Solution

Everything comes from the passing column. Parts (2) and (4) are lookups; part (3) needs three interpolations. The one decision that is easy to get wrong is that $D_{10}$ falls between the No. 10 and No. 40 sieves — a nearly fivefold gap on a log axis — so interpolating linearly there is a real error, not a rounding one.

1. Plot the GSD — percent passing on a linear vertical axis against opening on a **logarithmic** horizontal axis, nine points, with vertical reference lines at $4.75\ \mathrm{mm}$ and $0.075\ \mathrm{mm}$; this is `FiguresGeneral/GSD_plot.svg`
2. Soil fractions — gravel is what the No. 4 sieve retains, $G_F = 100 - P_{P,\#4} = 100 - 27 = \mathbf{73\ \%}$; fines are what the No. 200 sieve passes, $F_F = P_{P,\#200} = \mathbf{4\ \%}$; sand is the remainder, $S_F = 100 - 73 - 4 = \mathbf{23\ \%}$
3. $D_{60}$ and $D_{10}$ — using $\log(D_x/d_i) = \dfrac{\log(d_i/d_{i+1})}{P_{P,i}-P_{P,i+1}}\,(x - P_{P,i})$, with $60\ \%$ bracketed by $38\ \mathrm{mm}$ ($70\ \%$) and $19\ \mathrm{mm}$ ($49\ \%$), and $10\ \%$ bracketed by $2.00\ \mathrm{mm}$ ($20\ \%$) and $0.425\ \mathrm{mm}$ ($8\ \%$): $D_{60} = 38 \times 10^{-0.1434} = 27.3\ \mathrm{mm}$ and $D_{10} = 2.00 \times 10^{-0.5605} = 0.550\ \mathrm{mm}$
4. Coefficient of uniformity — $C_u = \dfrac{D_{60}}{D_{10}} = \dfrac{27.3}{0.550} = \mathbf{49.7}$
5. $D_{30}$ and the coefficient of curvature — $30\ \%$ is bracketed by $9.5\ \mathrm{mm}$ ($36\ \%$) and $4.75\ \mathrm{mm}$ ($27\ \%$), giving $D_{30} = 9.5 \times 10^{-0.2007} = 5.98\ \mathrm{mm}$, so $C_c = \dfrac{D_{30}^2}{D_{10}D_{60}} = \dfrac{(5.98)^2}{0.550 \times 27.3} = \dfrac{35.8}{15.0} = \mathbf{2.38}$
6. Median grain size — $50\ \%$ is bracketed by $38\ \mathrm{mm}$ ($70\ \%$) and $19\ \mathrm{mm}$ ($49\ \%$): $D_{50} = 38 \times 10^{-0.2867} = \mathbf{19.6\ \mathrm{mm}}$
7. Classify the gradation — the coarse fraction is gravel ($G_F = 73\ \%$ against $S_F = 23\ \%$), so the gravel limits apply: $C_u = 49.7 > 4$ **and** $1 < C_c = 2.38 < 3$, therefore the soil is a **well-graded gravel**
8. Validate — $D_{50} = 19.6\ \mathrm{mm}$ is barely above the $19\ \mathrm{mm}$ sieve, which passes $49\ \%$, exactly as it must; $D_{10} = 0.550\ \mathrm{mm}$ lies between the No. 40 and No. 10 openings, and $D_{30} = 5.98\ \mathrm{mm}$ between the No. 4 and $9.5\ \mathrm{mm}$ sieves, so all three sit inside their bracketing pair; the three fractions sum to $100\ \%$; and a $C_u$ near 50 spanning $0.550$ to $27.3\ \mathrm{mm}$ is consistent with the smooth, shoulderless curve in `GSD_plot.svg`

## Verification

```python
import math

def logterp(x, di, Pi, dj, Pj):
    """Deck formula: log(Dx/di) = log(di/dj)/(Pi-Pj) * (x-Pi).  i = coarser sieve."""
    return di * 10 ** (math.log10(di / dj) / (Pi - Pj) * (x - Pi))

d8 = [75.0, 38.0, 19.0, 9.5, 4.75, 2.0, 0.425, 0.15, 0.075]
P8 = [100., 70., 49., 36., 27., 20., 8., 5., 4.]
GF, FF = 100 - P8[4], P8[8]
SF = 100 - GF - FF
D = {}
for x in (10, 30, 50, 60):
    for (di, Pi), (dj, Pj) in zip(zip(d8, P8), list(zip(d8, P8))[1:]):
        if Pj <= x <= Pi:
            D[x] = logterp(x, di, Pi, dj, Pj)
            break
Cu = D[60] / D[10]
Cc = D[30] ** 2 / (D[10] * D[60])
```

Every value was computed from the stated percent-passing table, and the sieve-number conversion was then checked independently against the deck's own figure: the nine data markers in `FiguresGeneral/GSD_plot.svg` were extracted and decoded through that figure's axis calibration (log $x$: $10^2$ at device 50.87, $10^{-2}$ at 525.13; linear $y$: $0\ \%$ at 278.728, $100\ \%$ at 46.8396), returning $75,\ 38,\ 19,\ 9.5,\ 4.75,\ 2.0,\ 0.425,\ 0.15,\ 0.075\ \mathrm{mm}$ at $100,\ 70,\ 49,\ 36,\ 27,\ 20,\ 8,\ 5,\ 4\ \%$ — the stated table exactly, and confirmation that the ASTM openings now printed in the slide's header are the ones the figure was drawn with. The interpolation formula was verified at both endpoints (substituting $x = P_{P,i}$ returns $d_i$; $x = P_{P,i+1}$ returns $d_{i+1}$) so its sign and orientation are right. Each $D_x$ was additionally checked to lie strictly between its bracketing sieve openings, which catches an inverted interpolation that arithmetic alone would not. Units: all $D$ values in millimetres, both coefficients dimensionless, no conversion anywhere. Magnitude check: $73\ \%$ gravel with $D_{50} \approx 20\ \mathrm{mm}$ describes a coarse gravel with a sandy tail; $C_u \approx 50$ means the particle sizes span nearly two orders of magnitude, which is what a $0.550$-to-$27.3\ \mathrm{mm}$ range across $D_{10}$ to $D_{60}$ says directly, and $C_c = 2.38$ within $(1,3)$ confirms the curve rises smoothly with no gap — both consistent with `GSD_plot.svg`.

## Scaffolding decisions

The sieve-number conversion is no longer a step. With the openings printed in the slide's own table header, converting is reading rather than deciding, and a step whose whole content is a lookup the student can see would violate the governing rule. What was lost is the warning that a *bigger* sieve number means a *smaller* opening — that survives as the red note on step 1, where it still matters for plotting.

Step 1 has no blank. `GSD_plot.svg` goes in a `.sol-fig` on the second slide with the plotting rules restated in black, exactly as in Example 2.5. Repeating the rules rather than cross-referencing is intentional: this is the third time students meet them and the second time they are asked to draw one.

Step 2 produces three numbers but is one step, because it is one fact — the composition of the soil. All three are green. The $100-P_P$ versus $P_P$ contrast that Example 2.5 introduced is exercised again here without a fresh note; the note budget is spent instead on the interpolation in step 3 and the classification in step 7.

Steps 3 and 5 are where the interpolation happens, and they are split so that $C_u$ can be computed and interpreted before $C_c$ arrives. Step 3 pre-prints the formula in black and blanks the two substitutions and the two results — four blue values. Both brackets are pre-printed in black, because *identifying* the bracketing pair from a nine-row table is a reading task and the formula is unusable until it is done; putting it in the blank would mean a student who picks the wrong pair produces a number with nothing to check it against.

Step 4's blank is the substitution $\frac{27.3}{0.550}$ rather than the result, so the student has to put the larger size on top. $C_u$ is green.

Step 5 carries the heaviest single blank in the solution — $D_{30}$, then $C_c$ — and it is the only place where a squared quantity appears. The substitution $\frac{(5.98)^2}{0.550 \times 27.3}$ is blanked whole rather than split, because the error worth catching is dividing by $D_{10} + D_{60}$ or by $D_{60}$ alone. $C_c$ is green.

Step 6 reuses the same bracket as $D_{60}$, and that is worth noticing rather than hiding: two of the four $D$ values come from the single segment between $19$ and $38\ \mathrm{mm}$. Only the result is blue-then-green; the substitution is pre-printed, since it differs from step 3 in one digit.

Step 7 is the classification and its answer is a word. Both gravel criteria must be satisfied, so the step shows both tests explicitly with the verdicts in blue and the classification in green. The threshold pair chosen ($C_u > 4$, not $6$) depends on step 2, which is why the fractions come first.

Step 8 is black with the three bracket checks in blue.

Red notes, at most one per step: (1) plot on a log axis, or the whole fine half of the curve collapses into the left edge — and note that a bigger sieve number is a smaller opening, so the table already reads coarse to fine, left to right; (3) $D_{10}$ falls in the widest gap in the table, from $2.00$ down to $0.425\ \mathrm{mm}$, so interpolating linearly there gives $0.688\ \mathrm{mm}$ instead of $0.550\ \mathrm{mm}$ and drops $C_u$ from $49.7$ to $39.7$; (5) $C_c$ needs $D_{30}$ squared over the *product* of the other two, never their sum; (7) both tests must pass for well-graded, and the thresholds are the gravel ones because the soil is $73\ \%$ gravel.

The step-3 note is the most valuable one in the example and is also the longest; it carries a `data-slide` short form: "Linear interpolation here gives 0.688 mm, not 0.550 — the gap is 2.00 to 0.425 mm."
