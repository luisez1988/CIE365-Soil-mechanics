## Statement

> A permeable water channel will be constructed as shown in the figure. Three layers of sand with $k_1 = 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$ cm/s, $k_2 = 7.89 \times 10^{-3}$ cm/s, and $k_3 = 1.2 \times 10^{-4}$ cm/s connects a river and the proposed channel as illustrated. Calculate the hydraulic gradient between the river and the channel, the equivalent permeability of the layers, and the flow per unit of length.

## Given / Find

- Layer thicknesses measured perpendicular to the bedding, read from `FiguresGeneral/channels.svg`: Soil 1 (SC) 3 ft, Soil 2 (SP) 3 ft, Soil 3 (SM) 4 ft, total $b = 10\ \mathrm{ft}$
- $k_1 = 1.5 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{cm/s}$ (SC), $k_2 = 7.89 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm/s}$ (SP), $k_3 = 1.2 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{cm/s}$ (SM)
- River water surface at Elv. 137.23 ft, proposed channel water surface at Elv. 125.5 ft, horizontal separation 1255.6 ft
- The base of the layered package runs from Elv. 128.5 ft at the river to Elv. 115.5 ft at the channel; below it lies an impermeable CH clay over impermeable rock
- **Find:** $i$, $k_{eq}$, and $q$ per unit length of channel

## Assumptions

The flow runs **parallel to the layering**, from the river to the channel, so the three layers share the same head difference and carry different discharges. That makes them a parallel system, $k_{eq} = \dfrac{\sum k_i A_i}{A}$, and with a constant width it reduces to a thickness-weighted average.

The channel is cut through all three layers, as drawn, so every layer discharges into it. If the channel bottomed out inside Soil 1, only that layer would drain and the answer would collapse to $k_1$.

The flow path is taken along the layers, which drop 13 ft over the 1255.6 ft of horizontal separation. The resulting path length is 1255.7 ft. Treating the path as horizontal instead changes $i$ by 0.005 %, so the distinction does not survive to the answer, but it is worth doing once so students see that the flow path is not the horizontal distance in general.

Both water bodies are steady, so the head difference is constant and this is a steady-state calculation.

The CH clay and the rock beneath it are impermeable, so no flow leaves the bottom of the package and the full 10 ft carries the discharge.

"Per unit of length" means per unit length measured along the channel, perpendicular to the section drawn.

## Solution

Water leaves the river, crosses the three sand layers side by side, and enters the channel. Layers in parallel share $\Delta h$ and $L$, so their discharges add and the equivalent permeability is the thickness-weighted average.

1. Head difference — $\Delta h = 137.23 - 125.5 = \mathbf{11.73\ \mathrm{ft}}$
2. Flow path length — the layers drop $128.5 - 115.5 = 13\ \mathrm{ft}$ over $1255.6\ \mathrm{ft}$ of horizontal run, so $L = \sqrt{1255.6^2 + 13^2} = 1255.7\ \mathrm{ft}$
3. Hydraulic gradient — $i = \dfrac{\Delta h}{L} = \dfrac{11.73}{1255.7} = \mathbf{9.34 \times 10^{-3}}$ (dimensionless)
4. Equivalent permeability — with a unit width the areas are proportional to the thicknesses, so $k_{eq} = \dfrac{\sum k_i b_i}{\sum b_i} = \dfrac{(1.5\times10^{-5})(3) + (7.89\times10^{-3})(3) + (1.2\times10^{-4})(4)}{10} = \mathbf{2.42 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm/s}}$
5. Flow area per unit length — the full thickness carries the flow: $A = 10\ \mathrm{ft} \times 1 = 304.8\ \mathrm{cm}$ of face height per cm of channel length
6. Discharge — $q = k_{eq}\,i\,A = (2.42\times10^{-3})(9.34\times10^{-3})(304.8) = 6.89 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm^3/s}$ per cm of channel, which is $\mathbf{6.89 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{L/s}}$ per metre, or $\mathbf{59.5\ \mathrm{L/day}}$ per metre

Every value recomputed independently in Python. The unit handling in step 6 deserves a second look because it mixes systems on purpose: $k_{eq}$ and $i$ come out of the data in cm/s and dimensionless respectively, so the area must be expressed in centimetres before they meet, and $10\ \mathrm{ft} = 304.8\ \mathrm{cm}$ does that. The conversions at the end are $1\ \mathrm{m} = 100\ \mathrm{cm}$ and $1\ \mathrm{L} = 1000\ \mathrm{cm^3}$, then $86\,400\ \mathrm{s/day}$. Magnitude check: $k_{eq} = 2.42 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm/s}$ sits between $k_2$ and $k_3$ and much nearer the SP layer, which is right for a parallel system, where the most permeable layer dominates. Sanity on the result: 60 litres a day per metre of channel is a seepage loss a designer would accept, and it is small enough to be believable for a 10 ft thick sand package across a quarter of a mile.

## Scaffolding decisions

Step 1 is blue on the substitution and the value. It is arithmetic, but deciding which two of the four elevations on the figure are the water surfaces is not.

Step 2 is black on the reasoning, blue on the two lengths and the result. Many students will write 1255.6 here and be right to within rounding; the step exists so that they know why they were right.

Step 3 is blue on the substitution, green on $i$.

Step 4 is black on the symbolic form of $k_{eq}$, blue on the whole substitution, green on $k_{eq}$. The substitution is long but it is not blanked as a single span; each $k_i b_i$ product is its own blank, so pairing the right permeability with the right thickness is the decision being made.

Step 5 is blue on the conversion. Step 6 is blue on the substitution and green on each of the three reported forms of the answer.

Red notes: that parallel layers share the head difference while series layers share the discharge, which is the fork this example turns on; that the thickness-weighted average is dominated by the most permeable layer, so the SC layer barely matters; that $k$ in cm/s forces the area into $\mathrm{cm^2}$ before the multiplication; and that "per unit of length" here means per unit length along the channel, so the answer is a discharge per unit length rather than a discharge.

The note on step 4 needs a `data-slide` short form. The full text, which contrasts the thickness-weighted average of the parallel case against the resistance sum of the series case in Example 5.8, is far past the slide budget and is worth keeping in the book.

No note may quote $k_{eq}$; the step 4 note says the answer is dominated by one layer, not which value it lands on.

## Flags

The legacy solution computes the flow path as $\sqrt{1255.6^2 + 11.73^2}$, using the head difference as the vertical rise rather than the 13 ft drop of the layers. Both give the same $i$ to four significant figures, so nothing moves, but the new solution uses the layer geometry because that is the physical flow path. Worth a look in case the 13 ft reading of the figure is not what was intended.

The four elevations on the figure are easy to confuse. Elv. 137.23 and Elv. 125.5 are the two water surfaces; Elv. 128.5 and Elv. 115.5 are the base of the sand package at each end. The solution says so explicitly under Given, and the slide should too.

The SC layer contributes 0.2 % of the total transmissivity. That is worth a closing remark, since it is the reason a designer would spend the site investigation budget on the SP layer.

The step that looks wrong but is not is going from $\mathrm{cm^3/s}$ per cm to $\mathrm{L/s}$ per m. Two conversions run in opposite directions and nearly cancel: the volume shrinks by 1000 going from $\mathrm{cm^3}$ to L, while the length grows by 100 going from cm to m, so the net factor is $	imes 100 / 1000 = 	imes 0.1$, not $	imes 1000$ or $\div 1000$. That single factor of ten is what makes $6.89 	imes 10^{-3}$ become $6.89 	imes 10^{-4}$ while the leading digits stay put, which is exactly the coincidence that makes the line look like a typo. Then $\mathrm{L/s}$ to $\mathrm{L/day}$ is $	imes 86\,400$ with nothing to cancel it.
