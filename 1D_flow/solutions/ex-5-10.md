## Statement

> Consider an aquifer with three horizontal layers as listed in the table. The head at the top of the top layer is 101.3 m and the head at the bottom of the bottom layer is 96.2 m. 1. Sketch the system 2. Calculate the equivalent horizontal and vertical hydraulic conductivities for the composite aquifer 3. Calculate the vertical component of the flow rate 4. Use Darcy's law to calculate the total head a the top, middle, and bottom of each individual layer

## Given / Find

| | Thickness (m) | $k_x$ (m/d) | $k_y$ (m/d) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Layer 1 | 1.5 | 6 | 0.1 |
| Layer 2 | 3.9 | 0.5 | 0.02 |
| Layer 3 | 2.75 | 2.5 | 0.05 |

- $h$ at the top of Layer 1 $= 101.3\ \mathrm{m}$, $h$ at the base of Layer 3 $= 96.2\ \mathrm{m}$
- **Find:** $k_{x,eq}$, $k_{y,eq}$, the vertical flow rate, and $h$ at the top, middle and base of each layer

## Assumptions

Layer 1 is the top layer and Layer 3 the bottom one, in the order the table lists them. The two given heads then bracket the stack and the flow is downward, since 101.3 m at the top exceeds 96.2 m at the base.

Each layer is homogeneous, and the contacts between them are horizontal and continuous, so the vertical discharge through every layer is the same and the horizontal gradient is the same in every layer.

The vertical flow rate asked for in part 3 is a discharge per unit plan area, the specific discharge $q = k_{y,eq}\,i_y$. Its units are $\mathrm{m^3/d}$ per $\mathrm{m^2}$, which reduce to $\mathrm{m/d}$. The statement says "flow rate" without giving a plan area, so per unit area is the only reading that closes.

The problem is one-dimensional and vertical for parts 3 and 4. $k_{x,eq}$ is computed because part 2 asks for it, but it plays no part in the vertical calculation. The horizontal gradient is never given, so no horizontal discharge can be computed and none is reported.

Steady state, so the same $q$ passes through all three layers and the head losses add up to the full 5.1 m.

## Solution

Vertical flow crosses the layers one after another, so they act in **series**: the same discharge passes through each and the head losses add, which gives $k_{y,eq} = \dfrac{L}{\sum L_i/k_i}$. Horizontal flow runs along them side by side, so they act in **parallel** and the discharges add, giving $k_{x,eq} = \dfrac{\sum k_i L_i}{L}$. Everything else follows from Darcy's law applied layer by layer.

1. Sketch and total thickness — three horizontal layers stacked with Layer 1 on top, the head fixed at 101.3 m on the upper face and 96.2 m on the lower face, and flow directed downward; $L = 1.5 + 3.9 + 2.75 = 8.15\ \mathrm{m}$
2. Equivalent horizontal conductivity — parallel: $k_{x,eq} = \dfrac{\sum k_{x,i} L_i}{L} = \dfrac{(6)(1.5) + (0.5)(3.9) + (2.5)(2.75)}{8.15} = \dfrac{17.825}{8.15} = \mathbf{2.19\ \mathrm{m/d}}$
3. Equivalent vertical conductivity — series: $\sum \dfrac{L_i}{k_{y,i}} = \dfrac{1.5}{0.1} + \dfrac{3.9}{0.02} + \dfrac{2.75}{0.05} = 15 + 195 + 55 = 265\ \mathrm{d}$, so $k_{y,eq} = \dfrac{8.15}{265} = \mathbf{3.08 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m/d}}$
4. Vertical gradient — $i_y = \dfrac{\Delta h}{L} = \dfrac{101.3 - 96.2}{8.15} = \dfrac{5.1}{8.15} = 0.626$ (dimensionless)
5. Vertical flow rate — $q = k_{y,eq}\,i_y = (3.08 \times 10^{-2})(0.626) = \mathbf{1.92 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m^3/d}}$ per $\mathrm{m^2}$
6. Head loss across each layer — the same $q$ crosses all three, so Darcy's law rearranged gives $\Delta h_i = \dfrac{q L_i}{k_{y,i}}$: $\Delta h_1 = \dfrac{(1.92\times10^{-2})(1.5)}{0.1} = 0.289\ \mathrm{m}$, $\Delta h_2 = \dfrac{(1.92\times10^{-2})(3.9)}{0.02} = 3.753\ \mathrm{m}$, $\Delta h_3 = \dfrac{(1.92\times10^{-2})(2.75)}{0.05} = 1.058\ \mathrm{m}$
7. Total head through the profile — start at 101.3 m and subtract each loss in turn; the middle of a layer is half its loss below its top:

| Layer | $h$ at top (m) | $h$ at middle (m) | $h$ at base (m) | $\Delta h_i$ (m) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 101.300 | 101.156 | 101.011 | 0.289 |
| 2 | 101.011 | 99.135 | 97.258 | 3.753 |
| 3 | 97.258 | 96.729 | 96.200 | 1.058 |

## Verification

```

  B = 8.15 m
  kx_eq = 17.825/8.15 = 2.187117 m/d
  sum(L/k) = 265.0 d ; ky_eq = 8.15/265.0 = 3.075472e-02 m/d
  anisotropy kx_eq/ky_eq = 71.1
  i = 5.1/8.15 = 0.625767 ; q = ky_eq*i = 1.924528e-02 m3/d per m2
  Layer 1: dH = q L/k = 1.924528e-02*1.5/0.1 = 0.2887 m | top=101.300 mid=101.156 bottom=101.011
  Layer 2: dH = q L/k = 1.924528e-02*3.9/0.02 = 3.7528 m | top=101.011 mid=99.135 bottom=97.258
  Layer 3: dH = q L/k = 1.924528e-02*2.75/0.05 = 1.0585 m | top=97.258 mid=96.729 bottom=96.200
  closes on 96.2? -> 96.200000
```

Every value recomputed independently in Python. The strongest check is the closure: the three head losses were computed one at a time from Darcy's law in each layer, and stepping down from 101.3 m lands on 96.200000 m, which is the boundary condition that was used only once, at step 4, to get $q$. The three losses sum to 5.100 m, as they must. Units check on every step: $L_i/k_{y,i}$ has units of days, so $k_{y,eq}$ comes out in m/d; $i_y$ is dimensionless; $q$ inherits m/d and is read as $\mathrm{m^3/d}$ per $\mathrm{m^2}$. Magnitude check: $k_{x,eq}/k_{y,eq} = 71$, so the composite aquifer is about seventy times more transmissive along the bedding than across it, which is the order of anisotropy layered alluvium normally shows.

## Scaffolding decisions

Step 1 carries no numerical blanks beyond $L$. The sketch is asked for by the statement and is best drawn rather than blanked, so the slide shows it and the blank is the total thickness only.

Steps 2 and 3 are the heart of the example and are blanked the same way: black on the symbolic form, blue on every individual term of the substitution, green on the result. Each $k_i L_i$ product in step 2 and each $L_i/k_i$ quotient in step 3 is a separate blank, so the student has to pair the right thickness with the right conductivity six times. Getting the two formulas the wrong way round is the classic failure here, and separate blanks make that failure visible instead of hidden inside one long span.

Step 4 is blue on the substitution, black on the equation. Step 5 is blue on the substitution, green on $q$.

Step 6 gives the symbolic rearrangement in black and blanks the three substitutions and values in blue. The three are kept on one step because they are the same operation three times.

The step 7 table is blanked by column: heads blue, losses carried down from step 6 in blue, with the 101.300 in the first cell pre-printed black because it is given.

Red notes: that vertical flow puts the layers in series and horizontal flow puts them in parallel, so the same three layers need two different formulas; that the series result is pulled down by the least permeable layer while the parallel result is pulled up by the most permeable one; that the vertical flow rate is a discharge per unit plan area because no plan area was given; and that the profile must land on the given base head, which is the check rather than a fourth answer.

The note on the series-versus-parallel fork needs a `data-slide` short form; the full text names both formulas and runs well past the slide budget.

The closing note gives the anisotropy ratio, 71, rather than restating either conductivity, so the build's answer-leak check has nothing to catch.

## Flags

Part 3 says "calculate the vertical component of the flow rate" without giving a plan area. The solution reports a specific discharge in $\mathrm{m^3/d}$ per $\mathrm{m^2}$. If a unit plan area was intended the number is the same and only the label changes, but the statement is worth tightening in the deck.

The table heads the columns $k_x$ and $k_y$, while the deck's own figures use $x$ for horizontal and $y$ for vertical and the text elsewhere uses $z$ for the vertical direction. Students working from both will trip over it. Not something this solution can fix, but the slide should say once, in black, that $k_y$ here is the vertical conductivity.

Layer 2 accounts for 3.75 m of the 5.1 m of head loss while being less than half the thickness. That is the result worth closing on, and it is the argument for why a thin low-permeability layer controls a vertical seepage problem.
