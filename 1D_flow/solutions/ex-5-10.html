Sketch the system Layer 1 on top, head fixed on both faces, so the flow is vertically downward: \(L=1.5+3.9+2.75=\) 8.15 \(\unit{m}\) Draw it first — and note \(k_y\) here is the vertical conductivity.
Horizontal conductivity — parallel Along the bedding the layers share \(i\) and their discharges add: \(k_{x,eq}=\dfrac{\sum k_{x,i}L_i}{L}=\) \(\dfrac{(6)(1.5)+(0.5)(3.9)+(2.5)(2.75)}{8.15}\) \(=\) 2.19 \(\unit{m/d}\) A thickness-weighted average, so the most permeable layer pulls it up.
Vertical conductivity — series Across the bedding the layers share \(q\) and their head losses add: \(\displaystyle\sum\dfrac{L_i}{k_{y,i}}=\) \(\dfrac{1.5}{0.1}+\dfrac{3.9}{0.02}+\dfrac{2.75}{0.05}\) \(=\) 265 \(\unit{d}\), so \(k_{y,eq}=\dfrac{L}{\sum L_i/k_{y,i}}=\) \(\dfrac{8.15}{265}\) \(=\) \(\ee{3.08}{-2}\) \(\unit{m/d}\) Same three layers, two different formulas, and the direction of flow picks between them: crossing the layers is series and adds \(L_i/k_i\); running along them is parallel and adds \(k_iL_i\). Series is dragged down by the least permeable layer, which is why it is so much the smaller of the two — about seventy times smaller here, and nothing in the data said "anisotropic". The layering manufactures it.
Vertical gradient \(i_y=\dfrac{\Delta h}{L}=\) \(\dfrac{101.3-96.2}{8.15}\) \(=\) 0.626 The head is fixed on the two outer faces, so this is the average across the whole stack, not the gradient in any one layer.
Vertical flow rate \(q=k_{y,eq}\,i_y=\) \((\ee{3.08}{-2})(0.626)\) \(=\) \(\ee{1.92}{-2}\) \(\unit{m^3/d}\) per \(\unit{m^2}\) No plan area is given, so this is a discharge per unit plan area — which is why it carries units of m/d.
Head loss across each layer The same \(q\) crosses all three, so \(\Delta h_i=\dfrac{q\,L_i}{k_{y,i}}\):   \(\Delta h_1=\) \(\dfrac{(\ee{1.92}{-2})(1.5)}{0.1}\) \(=\) 0.289,   \(\Delta h_2=\) 3.753,   \(\Delta h_3=\) 1.058 \(\unit{m}\) Layer 2 is under half the thickness and takes nearly three quarters of the loss. The tightest layer runs the problem.
