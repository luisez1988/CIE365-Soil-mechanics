Which water controls which point A sits above the CH clay, in the shallow system held up by the Elv. 100 m surface. B sits below it, in the sand that daylights under the lake at Elv. 200 m. The head at a point is set by the water body it is connected to, not by the nearest water surface drawn on the page. The clay separates the two systems, so which side of it a point lies on decides everything that follows.
Head at A \(\hp=\) \(100-65\) \(=\) 35 \(\unit{m}\), so \(h_A=z_A+\hp=\) \(65+35\) \(=\) 100 \(\unit{m}\)
Head at B \(\hp=\) \(200-56\) \(=\) 144 \(\unit{m}\), so \(h_B=z_B+\hp=\) \(56+144\) \(=\) 200 \(\unit{m}\) B is artesian: its pressure head is far larger than its depth below ground. Drill into it and the water rises.
Gradient across the clay \(h_B>h_A\), so flow is upward, B to A, through \(L_{AB}=\) 9 \(\unit{m}\): \(i=\dfrac{h_B-h_A}{L_{AB}}=\) \(\dfrac{200-100}{9}\) \(=\) 11.1 A regional aquifer runs at \(i\approx0.01\). A thin clay separating two reservoirs with very different heads is where the steep gradients live, and an upward one through an aquitard is the condition behind the heave problems dealt with later in the course.
Heads at C and D Both lie in the same confined sand, so both take that unit's head: \(h_C=h_D=\) 200 \(\unit{m}\), with \(\hp=\) \(200-45\) \(=\) 155 \(\unit{m}\) at each.
Gradient C to D, and the line \(i_{CD}=\) \(\dfrac{200-200}{500}\) \(=\) 0, so the piezometric line from C to D is horizontal at Elv. 200 m and there is no horizontal flow between them. Zero gradient means no flow. It does not mean no water pressure.
