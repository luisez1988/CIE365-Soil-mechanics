## Statement

> The water levels in the two wells 20 m apart are 18.4 m and 17.1 m. The time of breakthrough at well B using a tracer introduced at A is 167 hours. Calculate (a) the horizontal hydraulic gradient, (b) the average linear velocity, and (c) the hydraulic conductivity

## Given / Find

- $L = 20\ \mathrm{m}$ between holes A and B, $h_A = 18.4\ \mathrm{m}$, $h_B = 17.1\ \mathrm{m}$
- $\Delta t = 167\ \mathrm{hr}$ for the tracer to travel from A to B
- $n_e = 0.25$ — **read from the figure**, not from the statement
- **Find:** (a) $i$, (b) the average linear velocity $v_s$, (c) $k$

## Assumptions

The effective porosity $n_e = 0.25$ is printed on the figure `FiguresGeneral/example_field_perm.png` and nowhere in the text of the statement. Without it part (c) cannot be answered, so it has to be called out explicitly rather than absorbed silently.

The two water levels are total heads. The wells are open holes standing in an unconfined aquifer, so the water surface in each one is the piezometric level at that point and $h$ is read directly, with no need to separate $z$ from $h_w$.

The tracer travels the straight line between the holes, so the travel distance equals the well spacing, $20\ \mathrm{m}$. Real tracer paths are longer than the straight line, which makes the computed $v_s$ a lower bound and therefore $k$ a lower bound too.

The aquifer between the wells is homogeneous, and the gradient is uniform over the 20 m, so a single $i$ describes the whole path.

## Solution

A tracer moves at the true seepage velocity, because it travels through the pores rather than across the whole cross section. Darcy's law is written in terms of the Darcy velocity, so the two have to be linked by $v = n_e v_s$ before $k$ can be extracted from $v = ki$.

1. Total heads — the water level in an open well is the total head at that well, so $h_A = 18.4\ \mathrm{m}$ and $h_B = 17.1\ \mathrm{m}$ are used as they stand
2. Hydraulic gradient — $i = \dfrac{h_A - h_B}{L} = \dfrac{18.4 - 17.1}{20} = \mathbf{0.065}$ (dimensionless)
3. Average linear velocity — this is the seepage velocity, and the tracer measures it directly: $v_s = \dfrac{L}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{20}{167} = 0.120\ \mathrm{m/hr} = \mathbf{3.33 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{cm/s}}$
4. Darcy velocity — $v = n_e\,v_s = 0.25 \times 3.33 \times 10^{-3} = 8.32 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{cm/s}$
5. Hydraulic conductivity — from $v = ki$, $k = \dfrac{v}{i} = \dfrac{8.32 \times 10^{-4}}{0.065} = \mathbf{1.28 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{cm/s}}$

## Verification

```
======== Example 5.5 - tracer test ========
  i  = (18.4-17.1)/20.0 = 0.065000
  vs = 20.0/167.0 = 0.119760 m/h = 3.326680e-03 cm/s
  k  = vs*ne/i = 3.326680e-03*0.25/0.0650 = 1.279492e-02 cm/s
  check k in m/d = 11.0548 m/d
```

Every value recomputed independently in Python. The unit conversion in step 3 is $20/167\ \mathrm{m/hr} \times 100\ \mathrm{cm/m} \div 3600\ \mathrm{s/hr}$; carrying it explicitly is what keeps the cm/s answer honest. Magnitude check: $k = 1.28 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{cm/s}$ falls in the clean-sand band of the permeability table shown earlier in the deck, which is consistent with a productive unconfined aquifer that a tracer can cross in a week. The same number is $11\ \mathrm{m/day}$, a figure a hydrogeologist would recognise immediately, and that cross-check is worth keeping.

## Scaffolding decisions

Step 1 is black. It looks like it says nothing, and that is the point: the single most common error in this problem is subtracting elevations from the well levels as though they were pressure heads.

Step 2 is blue on the substitution, green on $i$, because it is answer (a).

Step 3 is blue on the substitution and on the m/hr value, green on the cm/s value, because it is answer (b). The conversion factor chain is pre-printed black — it is bookkeeping, and blanking it only wastes a blank.

Step 4 is blue throughout. It is the step students skip, and skipping it is the difference between a right and a wrong answer to part (c), so it has to appear as its own step rather than being folded into step 5.

Step 5 is blue on the substitution, green on $k$.

Red notes: that $n_e$ comes from the figure and not from the text; that a tracer reports $v_s$ while Darcy's law is written in $v$, so one of them must be converted; that $n_e \leq n$ because dead-end pores carry no flow; and that the straight-line path makes the answer a lower bound.

The note on step 4 carries the whole idea of the example and will need a `data-slide` short form: the full sentence explaining why multiplying rather than dividing by $n_e$ is correct here runs well past the slide budget.

## Flags

The statement does not give $n_e$; it is only on the figure. If the figure is ever replaced or dropped from the slide, the problem becomes unsolvable. Worth adding "$n_e = 0.25$" to the statement text in `index.html`.

The phrase "average linear velocity" is standard in hydrogeology and means the seepage velocity $v_s$, but the deck uses "true seepage velocity" on the slide immediately before this one. The solution should use both terms once, in the same line, so students connect them.
