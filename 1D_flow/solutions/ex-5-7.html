F lies below the datum, so its elevation head is negative. The datum can go anywhere, but it must then be used everywhere: moving it shifts every \(\hz\) and every \(h\) by the same constant and leaves every head loss untouched. Losses are what the apparatus actually measures.
Heads at the two ends Top free surface: \(h=\hz+0=\) 40 \(\unit{cm}\).   The downstream surface sits on the datum, so the head at the exit is 0 \(\unit{cm}\).
Above the soil, nothing is lost Standing water loses no head, so \(h_B=h_C=\) 40 \(\unit{cm}\) and the loss down to C is 0. The first 20 cm is free water. It changes \(\hp\) from point to point, but never \(h\).
Gradient through the soil \(L_{soil}=\) 25 \(\unit{cm}\) (C down to F), so \(i=\dfrac{\Delta h}{L_{soil}}=\) \(\dfrac{40-0}{25}\) \(=\) 1.6 The flow path is the thickness of the soil, not the distance between the two water surfaces.
Total head at D, then at F \(h_D=40-i\,(12.5)=\) \(40-1.6(12.5)\) \(=\) 20 \(\unit{cm}\);   \(h_F=40-i\,(25)=\) 0 \(\unit{cm}\) \(h_F\) returns the exit condition, which was never used to find \(i\). That is the check, not a fourth answer.

E is worth a second look too: the datum passes through it, so its elevation head is zero, but E is inside the soil rather than in the free water below, so its total head is not.
