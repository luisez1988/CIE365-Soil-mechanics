Start at the boundaries The standpipes at the ends read their own reservoirs, so both heads are given: \(h_A=280\unit{mm}=\) 28 \(\unit{cm}\), \(h_D=200\unit{mm}=\) 20 \(\unit{cm}\). Assumed: the connecting pipework and the end fittings are frictionless, so the whole 80 mm difference between the two reservoirs is lost inside the specimen. That is what lets the reservoir levels be used as the boundary heads at A and D with no calculation, and the 80 mm dimension on the figure is exactly that difference.
Hydraulic gradient \(L_{AD}=3\times60=\) 18 \(\unit{cm}\), so \(i=\dfrac{h_A-h_D}{L_{AD}}=\) \(\dfrac{28-20}{18}\) \(=\) 0.444 The cylinder is tilted, so \(L\) runs along the axis while \(z\) is measured vertically. Never mix the two.
Heads at the interior points Uniform soil and equal spacing, so the drop repeats: \(i\times6=\) 2.67 \(\unit{cm}\) per interval, giving \(h_B=\) 25.3 \(\unit{cm}\) and \(h_C=\) 22.7 \(\unit{cm}\)

Levels in the standpipes Each rises to the total head of the point it taps, above the datum: 28.0, 25.3, 22.7, 20.0 \(\unit{cm}\). A standpipe reads \(h\), never \(\hp\). The four tops fall on a straight line only because the soil is uniform.
Cross-sectional area \(A=\dfrac{\pi\phi^{2}}{4}=\) \(\dfrac{\pi(6)^{2}}{4}\) \(=\) 28.3 \(\unit{cm^2}\)
Flow through the specimen \(q=k\,i\,A=\) \(\ee{3.4}{-4}\times0.444\times28.3\) \(=\) \(\ee{4.27}{-3}\) \(\unit{cm^3/s}\) Darcy's law uses the gross area, not the area of the voids. A silty-sand \(k\) gives a few cubic centimetres per quarter hour.
