## Statement

> Unit weight is the connector between the weights and the volumes. Using phase diagrams **find an expression for the total unit weight $\gamma_t$** and **show that $Se = wG_s$**.

Self-contained: this is a derivation, so there is no data to find. The two answers are algebraic expressions, not numbers.

## Given / Find

- Definitions already established earlier in the deck: $e = V_v/V_s$, $S = V_w/V_v$, $w = W_w/W_s$, $G_s = \gamma_s/\gamma_w$, $\gamma_t = W_t/V_t$
- The soil is three-phase: solids, water, air, with $V_t = V_s + V_w + V_a$ and $W_t = W_s + W_w$ (air is weightless)
- **Find:** (i) $\gamma_t$ in terms of $G_s$, $S$, $e$ and $\gamma_w$; (ii) a proof that $Se = wG_s$

## Assumptions

**Air is weightless.** $W_a = 0$ is taken as exact. This is the standard idealisation and it is what makes $W_t = W_s + W_w$; it is worth saying out loud because the air still occupies volume $V_a = (1-S)e$, so air vanishes from the numerator of $\gamma_t$ but never from the denominator.

**The sample is scaled to $V_s = 1$.** No mass or volume is given anywhere in the statement, so this is legal — every quantity asked for is a *ratio*, and ratios are independent of sample size. This is rule 4 of the deck's own "useful steps" slide. Had a single mass or volume been given, this choice would not be available.

**$\gamma_w$ is left symbolic.** The result is required as an expression, so $\gamma_w$ stays a symbol rather than being replaced by $9.81\ \mathrm{kN/m^3}$.

## Solution

There is no governing equation to look up — the whole derivation is bookkeeping on a phase diagram, where the *only* connector between the volume column and the weight column is a unit weight. That is precisely the point the statement is making, so the structure of the solution is: fill the volume column from $e$ and $S$, cross over once using $G_s\gamma_w$, then read both answers off the completed diagram.

1. Set the scale — take $V_s = 1$, so every other volume becomes a pure number times $1$
2. Volume column — $V_v = eV_s = e$, then $V_w = SV_v = Se$, and $V_a = (1-S)e$, giving $V_t = V_s + V_v = 1 + e$
3. Cross over to weights — $W_s = \gamma_s V_s = G_s\gamma_w V_s = G_s\gamma_w$
4. Weight of water — $W_w = \gamma_w V_w = Se\,\gamma_w$, so $W_t = W_s + W_w = (G_s + Se)\,\gamma_w$
5. Total unit weight — $\gamma_t = \dfrac{W_t}{V_t} = \boxed{\dfrac{(G_s + Se)\,\gamma_w}{1 + e}}$
6. Water content off the same diagram — $w = \dfrac{W_w}{W_s} = \dfrac{Se\,\gamma_w}{G_s\gamma_w} = \dfrac{Se}{G_s}$, and cross-multiplying gives $\boxed{Se = wG_s}$
7. The same $\gamma_t$ written in terms of $w$ — substituting $Se = wG_s$ into step 5, $\gamma_t = \dfrac{G_s(1 + w)\,\gamma_w}{1 + e}$, which is the form to use when $w$ is measured and $S$ is not
8. Sanity limits — $S = 0$ gives $\gamma_d = \dfrac{G_s\gamma_w}{1 + e}$ and $S = 1$ gives $\gamma_{sat} = \dfrac{(G_s + e)\,\gamma_w}{1 + e}$, so the single expression in step 5 contains all three unit weights from the previous table

## Verification

Symbolic algebra was checked by evaluating both sides of every claimed identity on 100,000 random admissible states $(G_s, e, S)$, building the phase diagram numerically from $V_s = 1$ and comparing against the closed forms. `sympy` is not installed in this environment, hence the numerical route.

```python
for _ in range(100000):
    Gs = random.uniform(2.4, 2.9); e = random.uniform(0.3, 1.5)
    S  = random.uniform(0.0, 1.0); gw = 9.81
    Vs, Vv = 1.0, e                     # phase diagram with V_s = 1
    Vw = S*Vv; Vt = Vs + Vv
    Ws = Gs*gw*Vs; Ww = gw*Vw; Wt = Ws + Ww
    gt_dir = Wt/Vt                      # bookkeeping straight off the diagram
    w = Ww/Ws
    worst = max(worst,
        abs(gt_dir - (Gs + S*e)/(1+e)*gw),      # claimed gamma_t
        abs(S*e - w*Gs),                        # claimed identity
        abs(gt_dir - Gs*(1+w)/(1+e)*gw),        # gamma_t rewritten in w
        ...)
```

```
  gamma_t = (G_s + S e)/(1+e) * gamma_w   [from V_s=1 diagram]
  S e = w G_s                             [identity]
  gamma_t = G_s(1+w)/(1+e) * gamma_w      [same, in terms of w]
  S=0 -> gamma_d   = G_s gamma_w/(1+e)
  S=1 -> gamma_sat = (G_s+e) gamma_w/(1+e)
  max residual over 100000 random (G_s, e, S) states = 1.066e-14
```

Every expression in steps 5–8 was reproduced independently from the diagram rather than copied from the algebra, and agrees to machine precision. Dimensional check: $G_s$, $S$, $e$ and $w$ are all dimensionless, so $\gamma_t$ carries the units of $\gamma_w$ — force per volume — as it must. Example 2.3 provides a numerical confirmation on real data: there $Se = 0.4234$ and $wG_s = 0.4234$.

## Scaffolding decisions

Step 1 is the only step whose *answer* is a decision rather than a quantity, so the blue blank is the value $1$ and the black text carries the justification. This is the step students skip, and skipping it is why they get stuck.

Steps 2–4 are the core fill-in work: the volume column ($V_v$, $V_w$, $V_t$) and then $W_s$ and $W_w$ in blue. These are short symbolic expressions, ideal for blanks — the student has to decide whether $e$ multiplies $V_s$ or $V_t$, which is the actual learning.

The two boxed results in steps 5 and 6 are green. They are what the problem asks for. In step 5 the substitution $\frac{(G_s+Se)\gamma_w}{1+e}$ is short enough to blank entirely; the $=$ chain and the symbolic $W_t/V_t$ stay black so the route is visible before anything is filled.

Step 7's substitution is left black and pre-printed — it is one algebraic move on a result the student has just derived, so blanking it tests nothing. Only the final rearranged form is blue.

Step 8 is presented as a two-row HTML table ($S=0$ and $S=1$) rather than prose, with the two limiting expressions in blue. Framing it as a check rather than a new result is deliberate: it connects this example back to the unit-weight table two slides earlier.

Red notes, at most one per step: (1) why setting $V_s = 1$ is legal here and when it would not be; (3) $G_s\gamma_w$ is the unit weight of the *grains*, not of the soil; (4) air has zero weight but real volume — it leaves the numerator, never the denominator; (6) $S$ and $w$ both measure "how much water", one by volume and one by weight, and $e$/$G_s$ are the exchange rate between them.

Note (4) is the one most likely to run over the character budget; a `data-slide` short form of roughly "Air: no weight, but real volume — it leaves the top of the fraction, not the bottom" is the fallback.

Eight steps is at the top of the normal range. Steps 7 and 8 could be merged if the slide runs long, but they answer different questions — "what if I measured $w$ instead of $S$" versus "does this reduce to what I already know" — so they are kept apart unless the layout forces it.
